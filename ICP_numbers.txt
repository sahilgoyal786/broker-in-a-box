National data suggests there are hundreds of thousands of real estate brokerage firms in the U.S., and the large majority by count are independent, non‑franchise brokerages rather than big-brand franchises.

Big picture numbers
The National Association of REALTORS® reports over 360,000 real estate brokerage firms operating in the United States (latest Census-based stat referenced in NAR’s 2024 “Quick Real Estate Statistics”).
​

Research cited in a 2020s-era “Franchise vs. Independent” financial model paper notes that about 86% of real estate firms are independent, non‑franchised companies.
​

Using those together as a ballpark:

Total brokerage firms: 360,000+.
​

Independent/non‑franchise firms (≈86%): roughly 310,000+ independent brokerages.

Franchised or other models (≈14%): on the order of 50,000 offices/companies tied to franchise brands or other structures.

These are directional estimates, not exact current headcounts, but they’re the best national-level indicators available.

Why the big brands feel so dominant
Even though independents are the majority by firm count, a large share of agents and production sit inside the big networks:

NAR data has historically shown that brokers and broker‑associates are more often with independents, but sales agents are split more evenly between independent and franchised companies.
​

A recent analysis notes that about 40% of agents are affiliated with franchised companies, illustrating that franchises host many more agents per office on average.
​

For example (U.S. plus global networks):

Keller Williams: 1,000+ market center offices, 160k+ agents worldwide, and hundreds of billions in U.S. volume.

Coldwell Banker, Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices, RE/MAX, etc., each run thousands of franchise offices collectively and account for a large chunk of national transaction sides and volume.

So on the ground, you see brands like Keller Williams, Coldwell Banker, RE/MAX, Compass, etc., everywhere—not because they outnumber independents by office count, but because their offices are larger and cluster huge agent rosters and production.

How to frame this for your “Focus Forty” and agent conversations
If you’re talking to agents or designing CE content/Facebook ad hooks, you can truthfully frame it like this (in your own words):

Most U.S. brokerages by count are local, independent shops, but a big share of agents and closings sit inside a relatively small number of national franchise brands.

That means your opportunity set as an LO is two-layered:

Depth with big-franchise offices (a lot of agents and transactions under a few roofs).

Breadth across independents (they massively outnumber franchise offices and often want more personalized lender relationships).

What NAR tells us
From NAR’s 2023 and 2025 Profile of Real Estate Firms:

81% of all real estate firms are single‑office firms, and the typical single‑office firm has 2–3 full‑time licensees.

Across all firms, the median number of full‑time licensees is 3.
​

Only a minority of firms report “10 or more people” on staff (including licensees and employees), while the largest firms (3+ offices) have medians of 20–64 licensees.

Around 86–87% of all firms are independent, non‑franchised.

Putting that together:

Most independent brokerages are very small (often the broker + 1–3 agents).

Firms with 10+ agents are the exception, not the rule, and they’re disproportionately the multi‑office and/or franchise shops.

Reasonable working estimate for your purposes
Using NAR’s medians and distributions as guardrails:

Out of all independent firms, a large majority (easily 70–80%+) are likely under 10 agents.

That leaves perhaps 20–30% of independents in the 10+ agent range, and only a slice of those will be 20–30+ agent “mini‑franchises.”

So in plain language you can use in class:

“Most independent brokerages are small—often just a handful of agents. Only a minority of independents have 10 or more agents in-house; those larger independents are the ones that start to look and behave more like franchise offices.”
